# Why RBB exists

The [README](../README.md) has the short version, and [using.md](using.md) shows how to use RBB. Sources are cited in square brackets and listed under [References](#references).

## The idea

Coding agents write code faster than anyone can read it, and they describe their work in their own words. Those words are not evidence. Agents change code nobody asked them to change [[1]](#ref-1), their final reports leave out about nine actions in ten [[20]](#ref-20), and when a task cannot be done honestly, about half of runs cheat, most often by editing the tests that define done [[19]](#ref-19). A person approving a plan, or a reviewer reading a pull request, usually has only that account to go on, and it is written after the work by the party whose work it describes.

RBB's answer is to write down what was asked before anything is built, where the agent cannot change it, and to compare the result with that record instead of with the agent's account. The rest follows. The record has to be clear enough for the person to approve knowing what they approve, concrete enough to test, fixed once approved, and carried into the pull request so that a reviewer reads the change against the ask. The comparison is made by a checker that never sees the builder's conversation, and whatever needs no judgement is worked out by a program, not a model.

Plain Claude Code with plan mode has no such record. Its plan is never compared with the result, so nothing notices when the plan and the code part ways. RBB is stock Claude Code's features plus the record and what the record makes possible.

## The underlying ideas

1. **The person acts at the beginning and at the end.** They give the goal and approve the spec; at the end they read the result and say whether they got what they asked for. In between, the model works, and everything it decides is written down. The person is asked about the goal and their intention, never how to build, because a person who holds only a goal, asked a technical question, tends to take the recommendation, so the question yields a default rather than a decision [[90]](#ref-90).
2. **The goal comes before any solution.** A request that names a fix is asked what the fix is for, and the goal is written apart from the fix, so that the fix is judged against the goal instead of standing in for it. A request that is an error is written as what should happen instead.
3. **A record is fixed before the build, and the result is compared with it, never with the builder's account.** A check approaches human judgement only against requirements that existed before the work [[10]](#ref-10), and a check that reads the agent's own words is steered by them [[12]](#ref-12)[[13]](#ref-13). Registering outcomes in advance did not stop outcome switching in medical trials on its own; the comparison has to be made and shown [[22]](#ref-22).
4. **The model settles technical points by fixed criteria, and writes each decision down.** The criteria come in order: the goal and the person's constraints, then the project's own conventions, then the smallest change. Each decision is written with its reason, its implications and the other decisions it touches, and they arrive together in the spec. People choosing linked options one at a time rarely reach the best combination, and seeing the combined outcome changes that [[83]](#ref-83). Each decision is shown with the alternatives that were weighed and with what would reverse it, because a longer explanation alone raises acceptance of a recommendation whether or not it is right [[53]](#ref-53)[[54]](#ref-54), while a named alternative and a premise give the person something to disagree with. Showing alternatives for scrutiny is not asking the person to choose: the model still settles the point, and the person approves or says what to change.
5. **The minimum change, and nothing that was not asked for.** Unasked changes are the default [[1]](#ref-1), the quiet change types carry the most risk [[5]](#ref-5), and unnecessary edits can be found [[16]](#ref-16)[[17]](#ref-17). So impact is mapped before the build, separate issues become follow-ups, anything the request did not ask for is named in the spec, and every change answers to a line of it. Smallest means the least behaviour changed, not the fewest lines.
6. **The checker advises; people and structure gate.** Whether a change serves the spec is a judgement, and automated judgement is weakest on the middle cases that matter [[11]](#ref-11), so nothing blocks on the checker's verdict. What is enforced is structural: no pull request body is written until a check covers the current code. What needs no judgement, such as files outside the plan, changed tests, and whether the examples failed before the change, is worked out by a program, because rules held as checks hold far better than rules held as text [[15]](#ref-15).
7. **Two readers, two documents.** The spec is written for the person and the reviewer, saying why; the plan is written for the model, short and numbered. The spec is limited by what the person must judge rather than by its length: at most four judgment calls, past which the change is split into separate goals, because accuracy falls past four interacting variables [[92]](#ref-92) and errors rose from 4% to 26% as a message grew from one topic to four [[49]](#ref-49). Its prose is kept under about 1,200 words, past which the model is warned once, because an approval faster than the reading time is not a reading [[48]](#ref-48); a sure decision takes a short form so the reading goes to the judgment calls. The plan has no limit, since the builder works from it alone. People judge outcomes better than code [[47]](#ref-47), and they give a plan real attention where a stream of per-action prompts gets a reflexive yes [[43]](#ref-43)[[44]](#ref-44).
8. **Everything an agent reasoned is written once, by that agent, and never re-typed.** The scout, the planner, the advocate and the builder each save their own file; code adds what it computes in files of its own, and every page is generated from those files. This is what Anthropic's safeguard against reproducing reasoning requires, and it also means that no agent can rewrite another's record. The records live under `.rbb/<change>/`, committed with the change so the reviewer has them beside the code, and removed after the merge; a team that wants nothing of the process in the repository keeps them on the requester's machine and freezes the spec by hash instead.

## The problems RBB answers

RBB was built against seven problems in how people work with coding agents: drift from the ask; the spec rewritten to match the drift, so the goalposts move; sign-off without comprehension; review not anchored to the ask; ambiguity settled by guessing, with no executable definition of done; decisions taken in a vacuum, where the best-seeming answer to each question does not add up to the best project; and a backlog that grows with everything noticed along the way. Each section below gives what the evidence says, how RBB answers the problem, and the findings with their sources.

Every row was checked against its primary source. Where the kind of source matters, the finding says so in words: observational data, a small sample, a laboratory study, a vendor's own figures, a single case, or an inference. "Bearing" is a reading of what a finding means for a solution, not part of the finding; it is an inference unless the study itself tested a remedy. Most agent figures come from 2025 and 2026 models; rates will move, and the mechanisms matter more.

### 1. Drift from the ask

**Drift is common, and the agent's own account mostly omits it.** On 200 already-fixed issues, agents changed code anyway 32–64% of the time [[1]](#ref-1). A quarter of Claude Code pull requests contain refactoring, against 15% of matched human pull requests, and in a separate study of Java agent commits, mostly by Codex, just over half of refactoring sat in commits that did not say so; both are observational [[3]](#ref-3)[[4]](#ref-4). Of 77 divergent test-passing patches inspected by hand, 27% changed more behaviour than the reference fix [[2]](#ref-2). In 16–80% of failing runs, depending on the model, all five runs of a task failed with near-identical patches: the same misreading repeated [[7]](#ref-7). Agents' final reports omit about nine actions in ten, and the further execution drifted from the plan, the more the report resembled the plan; an LLM judging those reports was too lenient to use [[20]](#ref-20). Drift is therefore best found by comparing the result with something fixed before the build rather than from the agent's report, which is an inference; some unnecessary edits can also be found from the code and tests alone [[16]](#ref-16)[[17]](#ref-17).

**How RBB answers it.** The spec fixed before the build; the checker compares the diff with it, never with the builder's account; every change tied to an item of the spec or listed as not asked for; impact mapped before the build and shown on the page.

### 2. The spec rewritten to match the drift

**Goals get rewritten, and switches go uncorrected unless someone compares.** Given impossible tasks, frontier agents cheated in about half of runs, and over 79% of Claude models' cheating was editing tests [[19]](#ref-19). Trial authors declared 14% of the outcomes they added; 58 of 67 top-journal trials switched outcomes despite public registrations, and journals printed 23 of 58 correction letters [[22]](#ref-22). Products update specs to match code as a feature [[26]](#ref-26)[[27]](#ref-27)[[28]](#ref-28), and checkers read text that can change after the work; which revision they read is inferred [[29]](#ref-29)[[30]](#ref-30). So every change to what defines done has to show, and the difference between goal and result has to be put in front of the reader. Making tests read-only cut one model's cheating from 50% to 1% but another's only to 39%, read from the paper's charts; hiding tests entirely cost legitimate work [[19]](#ref-19).

**How RBB answers it.** The spec frozen at approval, by a commit that carries a trailer naming it, or by the hash of each of its files; the build, the check and the pull request refuse a spec whose files changed since; `/rbb:status` says when the code has moved past the last check, and the pull request is not written until a check covers the current code.

### 3. Sign-off without comprehension

**Approval is often a click; outcomes are judged better than code.** At one firm about half of pull requests got a silent approval [[40]](#ref-40), and users approve 93–97% of per-action prompts while still rejecting 39% of plans [[43]](#ref-43)[[44]](#ref-44). In a small study 14 of 24 people changed nothing in a first plan [[45]](#ref-45); in another, editing plans made correct ones worse and barely helped flawed ones [[46]](#ref-46). In one study of 15 people, judging test outcomes instead of reading code raised correct judgments from 0.40 to 0.84 [[47]](#ref-47). Asking reviewers to look for security vulnerabilities raised detection about eightfold [[51]](#ref-51); file order mattered for one of two seeded defects [[52]](#ref-52); asking for the person's own call before the machine's reduced over-reliance, mostly for people who enjoy effortful thinking, and was disliked; explanations raise acceptance, not accuracy [[53]](#ref-53)[[54]](#ref-54). No study found objectively measures a team's understanding of its AI-written code over time [[58]](#ref-58)[[59]](#ref-59)[[41]](#ref-41).

**How RBB answers it.** Approval of one page of behaviour, decisions and examples, revised on anything the person asks to change or clarify until they approve; a pull request that stands on its own for a reviewer who was not in the session.

### 4. Review not anchored to the ask

**Review is anchored to the code or to text written after the fact.** The documentation of the main review tools describes no check against the task [[60]](#ref-60); ticket checkers do not say which revision of the ticket they read [[29]](#ref-29)[[30]](#ref-30); one new product has the agent write the intent after the build [[67]](#ref-67). Trace links from requirements to code made people 24% faster and 50% more correct [[62]](#ref-62); links drawn by an LLM were unreliable for user-level requirements across whole projects [[63]](#ref-63)[[64]](#ref-64).

**How RBB answers it.** The pull request carries the spec and the review as files beside the code (or the review in its body), and its body maps each change to the item it serves and lists every difference from the spec in one line.

### 6. Decisions taken in a vacuum

**Most people decide linked questions one at a time unless the combination is computed for them.** Three in four people or more treat related decisions separately [[84]](#ref-84)[[85]](#ref-85). Choosing two linked decisions separately, even with both in view, 3.3% of people ended with the jointly best pair (5% of those picking either extreme); offered one choice between the combined outcomes computed as single results, 93% did; with each part's outcome listed separately, 36%, in a registered replication [[83]](#ref-83). Later choices drift toward defaults [[88]](#ref-88)[[89]](#ref-89); four interacting variables is the limit of what people handle [[92]](#ref-92); decision aids induce the more accurate strategy when it is at least as easy as the shortcut [[101]](#ref-101)[[102]](#ref-102). None of six coding tools examined documents showing how answers interact [[104]](#ref-104).

**How RBB answers it.** By not asking the person technical questions one at a time. The model's decisions arrive as one set, in the spec, each with its reasoning, its implications and the decisions it touches, so the person judges them with the whole picture at approval and again at review. The rare question the person does answer comes with the recommendation, exactly why in terms of the wider codebase, and what the answer changes elsewhere. The examples stand in for the computed consequence, as concrete inputs and results of the decision set together. That the numeric-gamble result carries over to software decisions is an inference, not a finding.

### 7. A backlog that grows with everything noticed along the way

**Noticing and filing are cheap; resolving is not, so the list only grows.** An agent reads far more of the codebase per change than a person would, so it notices more. In seven open-source projects, more than 90% of bugs left unfixed for over a year affected users' normal work, and 40% of their fixes were a few changes in one file [[123]](#ref-123): much of what lingers is cheap once someone looks at it, and much of it matters. A stale bot shrank the pile in 20 large projects, closing more pull requests and resolving them faster, but active contributors fell considerably after adoption [[124]](#ref-124). Practice converges on the same few moves: fix small things only within a tight bound [[126]](#ref-126), give every item a verdict and never defer [[127]](#ref-127), let what nobody touches expire [[125]](#ref-125) or keep no backlog at all, trusting that what matters comes back [[128]](#ref-128), and let a written bar decide what may stay [[129]](#ref-129).

**How RBB answers it.** Discovery stays cheap; keeping costs evidence. A defect the scout finds off the path to the goal is kept only at a written bar (wrong results, data loss, security, a crash on a reachable path, a blocker) and with its evidence, and nothing is done about it in the change. It is shown on the spec page, on the review and in the pull request body as found along the way, so it is not lost and not quietly fixed.

### What the evidence does not settle

- How often agents rewrite goals after the work: no study of it in real use was found.
- Whether any tool beats a near-zero-tool baseline (a written convention and a careful human).
- How well any detector finds drift by type, especially misreadings inside the asked scope.
- Whether review ordered by the ask raises detection of unasked changes.
- Whether any intervention keeps a team's understanding of its AI-written code from eroding.
- Whether approving closed choices on concrete values catches more wrong values than approving prose.
- Whether showing how answers interact improves the set of decisions people make for coding agents, and how to show combined consequences for software decisions, which are fuzzier than the numeric gambles the strong results come from.
- Whether batched or one-at-a-time clarifying questions work better for people directing coding agents: no human study compares them.

Four mechanisms here are new and have no direct evidence of their own: impact mapping before the build, the ordered criteria for decisions, the independent advocate that argues the other side of each decision, and the saving of each agent's reasoning by that agent alone. The evidence cited beside them is for the problems they address, not for them.

## How RBB puts the ideas to work

**A protocol for Claude Code, packaged as a plugin, with git holding the record.** It is built from what Claude Code already has: skills, agents and workflow scripts. It needs nothing else: no program of its own, no language runtime, no service. Everything worked out by code runs inside Claude Code's workflows, and everything written is a file under `.rbb/` in the repository. The commands are few: `/rbb:rbb` runs the whole flow, and `/rbb:goal`, `/rbb:approve`, `/rbb:build`, `/rbb:check`, `/rbb:pr`, `/rbb:clear` and `/rbb:status` run its parts.

A change runs from a goal, shaped with the person, through a spec frozen before the build, to a check against that spec and a pull request that carries it. [using.md](using.md) walks through one.

Each stage runs at the effort its work needs, set by the workflow and never inherited from the session, so the record can say which model decided what. The scout, which explores the code and settles the decisions, the advocate and the alternatives reviewer run at high effort; the build at medium; the agents that relay git output and read files back at low. The builder works from the frozen spec alone, never the conversation, so what it builds is what was approved.

Two agents that saw neither the decider's conversation nor its reasons work on every decision. The advocate reads the code and argues the best material case for deciding otherwise; a case that holds is shown as an alternative, and a decision the scout called settled becomes a judgment call. The alternatives reviewer then keeps only the options, the scout's and the advocate's, that a competent developer could defensibly have taken. So "settled, with no credible alternative" means that an agent tried to find one and a second agent agreed there was none.

What needs no judgement is worked out by code: which changes fall outside the plan, which planned place each change touched, whether the spec changed since approval, whether a check covers the current code, and the stage of every change. Git facts are read by two agents whose outputs must match before code acts on them.

## Where the record lives, and why

The records of a change live under `.rbb/<change>/`, beside the code. Each file is saved once by the agent that wrote its content: the goal, the scout's findings, the advocate's cases, the plan and the build log by their authors; the manifest, the meta files, the approval, the check and the pages by code through a recorder that copies exactly what it is given and is checked by reading the file back. The pages people read, `spec.md`, `review.md` and `pr.md`, are generated from those files, so what a person approves is exactly what was recorded, and what the reviewer reads is exactly what was checked.

In the default mode the records are committed. The approval is a commit that carries the trailer `RBB-Approved: <change>`, which is how every later step finds the frozen spec, and git's history shows that the spec was not changed after approval. The build commits with its log, the pull request step commits the check and the review, and the body links to the review at that commit, so the link works after the records are removed. After the merge, `/rbb:clear` removes the folder in one commit, under any merge strategy; the records stay in the pull request and in history.

A team that wants nothing of the process in the repository chooses mode `none`: `.rbb/.gitignore` keeps every RBB file out of git, the spec is frozen by the git blob hash of each of its files, kept in `approval.json` on the requester's machine, and the pull request body carries the review and the hashes. The build, the check and the pull request recompute the hashes and refuse a spec that changed.

This differs from the original RBB, which kept records as git objects under their own refs, outside the working tree. That kept the tree clean but needed a program to read and write the refs, hooks to guard them, and CI to compare them. This version has none of those, by design: it must run on a machine where nothing can be installed beyond Claude Code and git. Committed files under one folder are what git, GitHub and a reviewer already understand, and the two modes let a team choose between a record in history and no trace at all.

## Alternatives, and why not

| Alternative | Why not |
|---|---|
| Stock Claude Code, well configured | It has no record fixed before the build: nothing to compare the result with, nothing a reviewer can read as the ask, no way to show the spec against the code |
| Instructions alone | Nothing notices a skip; agents take a forbidden shortcut about half the time anyway [[108]](#ref-108) |
| The host as the record (issues, labels, CI) | Works for teams on a host only, takes several acts across terminal and browser per change, and the issue text stays editable |
| Capture the ask, review afterwards | Nothing is fixed before the build, so edited words are read as the ask |
| A program of its own, as the original RBB had | It must be installed, built for each system, and trusted; this version runs where nothing can be installed |
| A hosted service | Needs an account and a service for something git and the pull request already hold |
| The person restates the change before approving | Restating cut misunderstanding in one study that was not randomised [[57]](#ref-57), but it asks the person to repeat what they already asked for, on every change. RBB instead revises the page on any change the person asks for until they approve |

Several choices weigh one risk against another:

- **No ambiguity can be guaranteed away.** Agents find under half of the gaps experts raise [[78]](#ref-78). So the scout resolves or asks about every gap it finds, and writes its assumptions into the spec so the person can catch the ones it missed. It claims no more than that.
- **The model settles technical points on its own, and its recommendations can be wrong.** So the goal invites the person's constraints in their own words, the scout's decisions rest on the project's own conventions found in the repository, the advocate argues the other side of each, and every decision is shown with its reasons and is reversible at approval and at review. On a solo project with no reviewer, a wrong decision the person does not notice ships.
- **The recommendation comes with the question, and its reason matters most.** A recommendation shown with a question anchors the answer. So every question lists the real answers with what each would mean, and the reason for the recommended one in terms of the request. That is what lets a person see a wrong recommendation; it does not stop a right-looking one from anchoring.
- **"Doing more than requested is a failure" is enforced in several places, not by a gate.** Whether a change serves an item of the spec is a judgement, and the checker advises. So the rule binds the scout (follow-ups are recorded, never fixed), the builder (fix nothing the spec does not name), the check (every change labelled), and the pull request (every difference listed), rather than blocking on the checker's verdict.
- **Every agent's reasoning is saved by that agent, once.** Anthropic's models refuse to reproduce another agent's reasoning when asked to write it out. RBB does not work around that: nobody re-types what the scout, the planner, the advocate or the builder wrote, and the pages are generated from their files. The cost is a spec spread over several files; the page remains the one document a person reads.
- **The advocate adds reading, so it has a materiality bar.** Every case it raises becomes a judgment call, and a spec may carry four. Without the bar, a first run raised five cases on a one-function change and the spec had to be split. With it, the advocate argues only where deciding otherwise changes what the requester gets.
- **Records are kept until the merge, then removed.** The pull request and history keep them; the working tree does not.

RBB also leaves out, on purpose: menus of technical options; a program, a hosted service, accounts, signing keys and provenance dashboards; hooks; large spec documents, since the record is one page for the person and a plan for the builder; and a second model family.

## What RBB does not claim

Each item is open. Some would change with the work named; others are limits of the approach.

- **The model's own decisions can be wrong.** The criteria, the advocate, the recorded reasoning and reversal at review reduce this; they do not remove it, and on a solo project with no reviewer nothing does.
- **A record can be edited.** In committed mode, git history shows an edit after approval, and the build, the check and the pull request read the spec from the approval commit, never from the working tree. In mode `none`, the hashes in `approval.json` are the only evidence, and they live on the requester's machine. Nothing stops a person with write access from rewriting history.
- **Approval is an answer to a question.** RBB cannot tell whether a person gave it with understanding; whether people understand what they approve is untested here.
- **Examples can be special-cased.** The checker reads for it; input variants are not built.
- **The checker's reach is unmeasured.** Whether it finds a deviation planted in a change is untested, and its not-asked-for list has no measured false-alarm rate. Three development runs on one task are the evidence so far.
- **The checker is not fully isolated.** It never sees the builder's conversation, but it is a subagent in the same session and shares the machine. It runs only git commands, and whether a held or refused command stops it depends on the person's permission rules.
- **Impact mapping is only as good as the scout's reading.** Agents failed to read every file in 68% of file-review runs [[116]](#ref-116). The spec lists what was examined, so a reader can see what was not, but nothing records what the scout actually read, so a made-up list reads the same as a real one.
- **The minimum change is a rule, not a result.** Whether it is reached is untested; what the check can show is unasked and unnecessary edits, which are detectable [[16]](#ref-16)[[17]](#ref-17).
- **Comprehension over time.** The page and the pull request help within one sitting. Whether a team keeps understanding its code over time has no measured intervention anywhere [[58]](#ref-58)[[59]](#ref-59)[[41]](#ref-41).
- **Adherence and cost in real use.** Only weeks of use can show whether the process is kept on most behaviour changes, and whether its cost to the person is acceptable. The scout with the advocate takes about five minutes; a build, a check and a pull request body each a few minutes.
- **A wrong diagnosis becomes a wrong spec.** Real requests carry an error or a sentence, not a diagnosis, so the scout diagnoses. The page marks what it observed and what it inferred, with the evidence, and the builder runs each example before the change, so a wrong diagnosis fails its own example.
- **Untested paths.** Linux and macOS have not been run; every step has run on one testbed task, on Windows, by RBB's author.
- **Claude Code's own mechanics.** A command a permission prompt or a guarding tool holds makes a workflow wait silently until it is answered; RBB cannot tell a hold from a long step. An agent that passes a workflow its arguments as text is tolerated; one that paraphrases a page it was told to show as written is not caught.

The rates above come from 2025 and 2026 models and will move. The mechanisms are what the design rests on.

## Borrowed from other tools

Several mechanisms come from other tools. The checks that need no model are agent-spec's. The rule that the plan's wording cannot put a change in scope is BMAD's review. Challenging a contradicted directive once, and never recording bare assent as the person's decision, are compound-engineering's. Marking each decision sure or a judgment call, recording what the codebase settled, and the advocate that argues for the option not chosen are flow-next's and the original RBB's scope check. The checker's starting hypothesis, that the examples pass and the goal is missed, is GSD's verifier, and its "needs a person" label follows GSD and Atlassian Rovo Dev. Treating comments and commit messages as unverified claims, and the review focus, are superpowers'. Each is cited where it is used.
